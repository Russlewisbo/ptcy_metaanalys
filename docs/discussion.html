The Mechanistic Framework

The totality of findings across six pre-specified outcomes and two independent comparisons informs a mechanistic framework built on the distinction between two immunological consequences of PTCy.

Pathway 1: GVHD Suppression (Intended)

PTCy eliminates alloreactive T cells during the critical window of allorecognition, reducing acute GVHD grade II–IV across both comparisons. This propagates downstream:

  • NRM benefit shows steroid mediation in both comparisons
  • OS benefit is not separable from GVHD suppression: after steroid adjustment the estimate crosses the null (M2 OR 1.04 [0.89–1.22]). The previously claimed direct contribution did not survive cohort deduplication

Pathway 2: T-Cell Lymphodepletion (Unintended)

The same cyclophosphamide pulse depletes the nascent CMV- and bacterial-specific T-cell repertoire. This manifests as elevated infection risk — but the magnitude and direction depend on the comparator’s own depletion depth.

The depletion hierarchy makes three predictions, all confirmed:

ImportantThe Sensitivity Pattern Confirms the Hierarchy

  1. CMV converges to null (primary OR 0.98 → sensitivity 1). When PTCy arms also use ATG, both sides of the comparison have deep T-cell depletion. CMV rates equalise — exactly as the hierarchy predicts. If the C1 CMV excess were an intrinsic PTCy property (rather than a relative depletion effect), adding ATG to the PTCy arm should have made things worse, not better.

  2. aGVHD attenuates (primary 0.63 → sensitivity 0.78). Adding ATG to the PTCy arm reduces the differential in alloreactive T-cell depletion between arms, narrowing the GVHD advantage. This is the GVHD-pathway analogue of the CMV convergence.

  3. OS strengthens (primary 0.83 → sensitivity 0.74). PTCy+ATG combinations appear to improve survival more than ATG alone, possibly through redundant GVHD suppression from both agents. This may explain why the aGVHD and CMV effects attenuate (both arms are deeply immunosuppressed) while survival still improves.

Synthesis

The primary C2 CMV estimate alone no longer demonstrates a statistically credible direction reversal. However, the three-way pattern across the primary and sensitivity analyses — C1 harmful, C2-primary near-null, C2-sensitivity null — traces a dose–response curve along the depletion axis. The framework is better supported by this graded pattern than it was by the pre-Block-9 binary reversal, even though the individual C2 CMV CrI is wider.

Comparator ATG use moderates the C1 CMV effect

A further test of the depletion hierarchy exists within the C1 data itself. Although C1 comparator arms are defined as “CNI+MTX/MMF,” many also received ATG — and when they did, the CMV effect changes direction.

When the comparator arm uses ATG (k = 12), the pooled OR is 1.05 — essentially null, identical to the C2 pattern. When the comparator has no ATG (k = 7), the OR is 1.25, showing a PTCy-associated CMV excess. The moderator test is borderline (p = 0.501, R² = 1%).

This is the depletion hierarchy operating within a single comparison. The CMV excess attributed to PTCy is not an intrinsic PTCy effect — it is a relative effect that depends on how much T-cell depletion the comparator carries. This pattern was independently confirmed in the C2 expanded-arm sensitivity analysis (above), where adding ATG to the PTCy arm similarly converged CMV toward null.

CMV and donor/recipient serostatus

The depletion hierarchy predicts that PTCy should increase CMV reactivation most when CMV-specific T-cell reconstitution matters most — i.e., when the recipient carries latent virus (R+). We assessed this in two ways.

Meta-regression. Neither recipient seropositivity (k = 11, p = 0.72) nor D+/R+ prevalence (k = 8, p = 0.43) significantly moderated the PTCy effect on CMV when used as study-level ecological covariates. Both analyses were severely underpowered (only 8–11 of 22 C1 CMV studies report arm-level serostatus data).

Subgroup tabulation. Three studies (Chorao 2024, Goldsmith 2021, Steiner 2024) reported CMV reactivation stratified by donor/recipient serostatus, contributing one study per D/R combination:

D/R stratum PTCy % Comparator % OR Source
D+/R+ 47% 40% 1.34 Chorao 2024
D−/R+ 67% 69% 0.91 Chorao 2024
D+/R− 20% 11% 2.06 Goldsmith 2021
D−/R− 2% 1% 1.89 Goldsmith 2021
R+ (pooled) 51–68% 29–48% 2.4–2.6 Goldsmith 2021, Steiner 2024

The D−/R+ stratum is the only one where PTCy did not increase CMV risk. In this configuration, the recipient carries CMV-specific memory T cells from prior infection (R+) but the donor does not (D−). Post-transplant immune reconstitution against CMV therefore relies on surviving recipient memory cells. PTCy’s preferential depletion of alloreactive (recently activated) T cells may spare these quiescent, CMV-specific memory cells, whereas CNI-based regimens continuously suppress all T-cell function including memory responses. This is consistent with the mechanistic selectivity of cyclophosphamide for dividing cells.

In contrast, when the donor is seropositive (D+/R+ and D+/R−), the comparator arm benefits from donor-derived CMV-specific T cells during immune reconstitution — a pathway that PTCy disrupts by depleting the incoming donor graft during the early post-transplant window.

WarningHypothesis-Generating Only

All D/R stratum-specific estimates are based on k = 1 study each. The ecological meta-regressions lack power (k = 8–11 with restricted ranges). Testing whether PTCy’s CMV effect genuinely differs by D/R serostatus requires individual patient data (IPD) meta-analysis, where within-study D/R interactions can be estimated without ecological bias.

Chronic GVHD: The Dominant Treatment Effect

The C1 moderate-severe chronic GVHD OR of 0.47 [0.38–0.57] is among the largest treatment effects in this meta-analysis, exceeding the aGVHD effect (OR 0.67). This magnitude — roughly a halving — is clinically important given that moderate-severe cGVHD is the leading cause of late morbidity and long-term immunosuppression dependence after allogeneic HCT.

The C1-vs-C2 attenuation (OR 0.47 → 0.79) mirrors the aGVHD pattern (0.67 → 0.63) and is mechanistically coherent: ATG also depletes alloreactive T cells, narrowing the differential. However, PTCy retains a residual cGVHD advantage even against ATG (90% posterior probability of OR < 1), suggesting PTCy may target the chronic alloimmune process through a partially distinct mechanism — possibly the selective elimination of alloreactive T cells that are actively dividing during the early post-transplant window, which are disproportionately represented among the long-lived T-cell clones that drive chronic GVHD.

Infection-Related Mortality: The Risk-Benefit Resolution

The IRM analysis is arguably the most clinically important finding in this report because it resolves the central risk-benefit question: do the excess infections with PTCy translate into excess deaths?

The answer is nuanced. In C2 (vs ATG), both the Bayesian (OR 0.90 [0.74–1.09]) and frequentist (OR 0.81 [0.66–1.00]) models agree: PTCy does not increase infection-related mortality relative to ATG. In C1 (vs CNI+MTX), the two models now agree after cohort deduplication: the Bayesian estimate is OR 1.19 [1.00–1.43] and the frequentist RE model gives OR 1.03 [0.72–1.46]. Both are compatible with no increase. The previously reported Bayesian–frequentist divergence (1.35 vs 1.01) was driven by repeated EBMT ALWP publications entering the model as independent studies.

The clinically relevant interpretation is that even under the more pessimistic Bayesian estimate, the IRM increase (OR 1.19) is far smaller than the corresponding infection rate increases (CMV OR 1.26, BSI OR 1.87, BK virus OR 2.48). This disconnect between infection incidence and infection mortality suggests that PTCy-associated infections are, as a class, manageable with contemporary antimicrobial prophylaxis and pre-emptive therapy. The net survival effect remains favourable (OS OR 0.84).

Practical Implications

The expanded analysis — now covering 10 outcomes across 3 comparisons — carries direct clinical implications:

NoteATG Is Not the Solution

The C2 data show that ATG-based regimens carry at least as great a T-cell reconstitution liability as PTCy. The infection costs of PTCy are best understood as a general consequence of T-cell–depleting prophylaxis, not a PTCy-specific limitation. PTCy’s advantage over ATG lies in its superior cGVHD prophylaxis and preserved GVL activity.

Limitations

Context: relationship to prior meta-analyses

This review is substantially larger than prior work in this field. The largest comparative PTCy meta-analysis to date (Li et al. 2025, Ann Hematol) pooled 9 studies and 1,674 patients with only 4 studies contributing CMV data (RR 0.97 [0.76–1.22]); Tang et al. (2023, Front Oncol) included 6 studies from unrelated donors with CMV data too sparse for subgroup analysis (RR 0.89 [0.63–1.24]); Arcuri et al. (2019, BBMT) included 20 studies comparing haplo-PTCy vs URD but did not analyse infection outcomes; Gagelmann et al. (2019, JAMA Oncol) pooled 13 studies without infection data. A 2023 systematic literature review of PTCy vs ATG on CMV (Varma et al., Cancers) was qualitative only, concluding that data were scarce and no pooled estimate was possible. Thiruvadi et al. (2026, OFID, abstract) performed a proportion meta-analysis of csCMVi incidence after matched-donor PTCy but included no comparator arm. The present review (251 studies, 525 arms, 3,604 outcomes) therefore represents an order-of-magnitude increase in scale and, to our knowledge, the first meta-analysis to simultaneously pool infection (CMV, BSI, IFI) and transplant (GVHD, NRM, OS) outcomes across two pre-specified comparisons. This scale enables subgroup and meta-regression analyses that were not feasible in prior work. However, larger scale also amplifies certain limitations:

Specific limitations

  1. Observational evidence. Only 14 of 251 included studies (5.6%) are RCTs. This proportion is lower than in the smaller meta-analyses of Li et al. (2/9 RCTs, 22%) and Tang et al. (1/6, 17%), reflecting the broader inclusion criteria and the observational nature of the PTCy literature as a whole. All findings — including the depletion hierarchy — are therefore susceptible to residual confounding despite mediation modelling and multivariable-adjusted subsets.

  2. Sparse infection data. Despite the overall corpus size, only k = 6 C1 studies contribute to BSI and k = 6 to IFI. BK virus (k = 11 C1, k = 3 C2) is better represented but has not been meta-analysed in prior PTCy reviews. By comparison, Li et al. (2025) pooled 2 studies each for bacterial and fungal infection (RR 0.86 [0.57–1.29] and RR 0.23 [0.07–0.79], respectively). Our BSI estimate (OR 1.87 [1.33–2.62]) is directionally opposite to Li et al.’s bacterial infection RR, but the two analyses are not directly comparable: Li et al. studied PTCy vs ATG in haploidentical transplants, while C1 compares PTCy vs CNI+MTX across all donor types. The depletion hierarchy predicts exactly this divergence. Nonetheless, both estimates rest on very few studies, and dedicated prospective studies of infection after PTCy are urgently needed.

  3. Single-extractor methodology. Data extraction and ROB assessment were performed by a single reviewer (RL) for all 241 assessed studies. This departs from the Cochrane-recommended dual independent extraction used by, e.g., Tang et al. (2023), Arcuri et al. (2019), and Luo et al. (2024). A stratified spot-check by a second reviewer (BMT program director) with inter-rater reliability assessment (Cohen’s kappa) is planned before publication but has not yet been completed. The ROB assessments should therefore be interpreted with this caveat in mind.

  4. Relapse-related mortality now analysed. The post-Block-9 analysis includes RRM as a new outcome (C1 OR 0.84 [0.76–0.93], k = 38; C2 OR 0.79 [0.69–0.89], k = 10), resolving the previous limitation. The RRM benefit is steroid-independent (no M1→M2 attenuation), supporting the GVL preservation hypothesis. However, RRM is an aggregate outcome that conflates relapse incidence with post-relapse mortality; dedicated relapse incidence data would further refine the mechanistic picture.

  5. Publication bias. RoBMA (Robust Bayesian Model-Averaging) detected strong evidence of publication bias for C1 OS (BF = 10.9) and C1 aGVHD (BF = 14.1), with moderate evidence for C2 OS (BF = 5.2) and C2 aGVHD (BF = 4.1). After bias adjustment, all model-averaged effects collapsed toward the null (P(effect) < 50% for all 9 outcomes). No meaningful bias was detected for C1 CMV, NRM, BSI, or IFI (BFs < 1). No prior PTCy meta-analysis has applied model-averaged publication bias correction; Tang et al. (2023) assessed funnel-plot symmetry qualitatively, while Li et al. (2025) did not report publication bias assessment for infection outcomes. The RoBMA default priors are deliberately skeptical (Normal(0, 1) on log-OR, 50% prior mass on the null), and the prospective/RCT subgroup analysis (below) suggests these estimates may be overly conservative — but the bias signals for OS and aGVHD warrant caution in interpreting effect magnitudes.

  6. Observational dominance and design-associated confounding. Only 14 of 251 studies are RCTs, and very few contribute to the paired analyses (2 RCTs in C1 OS; 0 in any C2 outcome). A prospective-vs-retrospective subgroup analysis of C1 outcomes showed that OS estimates were concordant across designs (prospective OR 0.76 vs retrospective 0.78), while C1 CMV showed a complete direction reversal (prospective OR 0.68 vs retrospective 1.39, p = 0.056). Investigation revealed this reversal is driven by case-mix confounding — all 3 prospective studies used matched donors (0% haplo) and 2/3 had ATG in the comparator arm — rather than reporting bias. This underscores that apparent “study quality” effects in this literature may reflect systematic differences in donor type, conditioning, and comparator regimen rather than methodological rigour per se.

  7. Haplo proportion as effect modifier. Meta-regression identified haplo donor proportion as a significant moderator of the aGVHD treatment effect in both C1 (β = +0.005/%, p = 0.049, R² = 22%) and C2 (β = +0.013/%, p = 0.034, R² = 47%), and of C1 OS (β = +0.005/%, p = 0.003, R² = 26%). PTCy’s advantages over both comparators attenuate as haplo proportion increases, reaching null at ~100% haplo for aGVHD and OS. This is not confounded by conditioning intensity and is mechanistically coherent, but it means the pooled estimates — which average across a range of haplo proportions — may overstate or understate the effect for any specific clinical scenario. Prior meta-analyses restricted to haploidentical transplants (Li et al. 2025, Arcuri et al. 2019) were not affected by this heterogeneity but also could not detect it.

  8. M2 complete-case analysis. Post-Block-9 M2 models use complete-case analysis for steroid adjustment (dropping arms with missing steroid data), differing from the pre-Block-9 mi() imputation approach due to a brms API change.

  9. C2 CMV sensitivity caveats. The expanded-arm sensitivity conflates PTCy+ATG combination arms (which test a different clinical question — whether adding PTCy to ATG changes outcomes) with pure PTCy vs ATG comparisons. Luo et al. (2024) specifically meta-analysed ATG+PTCy combinations (14 studies) and found lower aGVHD without increased infection, consistent with our sensitivity findings. However, the convergence pattern should not be taken as evidence that PTCy+ATG combinations are clinically equivalent to ATG alone.

  10. Ecological bias in meta-regression. All moderator analyses (haplo proportion, CMV serostatus, comparator ATG use, letermovir era) use study-level aggregates as covariates. This introduces ecological bias — the study-level association may not reflect the individual-level relationship. Testing the depletion hierarchy at the patient level, and confirming whether D/R serostatus truly modifies the PTCy–CMV relationship, requires individual patient data (IPD) meta-analysis.

## The Mechanistic Framework {#sec-disc-asymmetry}

### Pathway 1: GVHD Suppression (Intended)

- **NRM** benefit shows steroid mediation in both comparisons

## The Sensitivity Pattern Confirms the Hierarchy

1. **CMV converges to null** (primary OR `r round(or_c2_cmv$or, 2)` → sensitivity `r if(!is.null(post_c2_sens_cmv)) round(median(exp(post_c2_sens_cmv$b_ptcy_binary)), 2) else "—"`). When PTCy arms also use ATG, both sides of the comparison have deep T-cell depletion. CMV rates equalise — exactly as the hierarchy predicts. If the C1 CMV excess were an intrinsic PTCy property (rather than a relative depletion effect), adding ATG to the PTCy arm should have made things worse, not better.

2. **aGVHD attenuates** (primary 0.63 → sensitivity `r if(!is.null(post_c2_sens_agvhd)) round(median(exp(post_c2_sens_agvhd$b_ptcy_binary)), 2) else "—"`). Adding ATG to the PTCy arm reduces the differential in alloreactive T-cell depletion between arms, narrowing the GVHD advantage. This is the GVHD-pathway analogue of the CMV convergence.

3. **OS strengthens** (primary 0.83 → sensitivity `r if(!is.null(post_c2_sens_os)) round(median(exp(post_c2_sens_os$b_ptcy_binary)), 2) else "—"`). PTCy+ATG combinations appear to improve survival more than ATG alone, possibly through redundant GVHD suppression from both agents. This may explain why the aGVHD and CMV effects attenuate (both arms are deeply immunosuppressed) while survival still improves.
:::

### Synthesis

The primary C2 CMV estimate alone no longer demonstrates a statistically credible direction reversal. However, the three-way pattern across the primary and sensitivity analyses — C1 harmful, C2-primary near-null, C2-sensitivity null — traces a dose–response curve along the depletion axis. The framework is better supported by this graded pattern than it was by the pre-Block-9 binary reversal, even though the individual C2 CMV CrI is wider.

### Comparator ATG use moderates the C1 CMV effect {#sec-disc-cmv-comp-atg}

A further test of the depletion hierarchy exists *within* the C1 data itself. Although C1 comparator arms are defined as "CNI+MTX/MMF," many also received ATG — and when they did, the CMV effect changes direction.

```{r}
#| label: setup-comp-atg

    atg_used == "Y" ~ "ATG",
    atg_used == "N" ~ "No ATG",
```

When the comparator arm uses ATG (k = `r fit_atg_sub$atg$k`), the pooled OR is `r sprintf("%.2f", exp(coef(fit_atg_sub$atg)))` — essentially null, identical to the C2 pattern. When the comparator has no ATG (k = `r fit_atg_sub$no_atg$k`), the OR is `r sprintf("%.2f", exp(coef(fit_atg_sub$no_atg)))`, showing a PTCy-associated CMV excess. The moderator test is borderline (p = `r sprintf("%.3f", fit_atg_mod$QMp)`, R² = `r sprintf("%.0f%%", fit_atg_mod$R2)`).

This is the depletion hierarchy operating *within* a single comparison. The CMV excess attributed to PTCy is not an intrinsic PTCy effect — it is a *relative* effect that depends on how much T-cell depletion the comparator carries. This pattern was independently confirmed in the C2 expanded-arm sensitivity analysis (above), where adding ATG to the PTCy arm similarly converged CMV toward null.

### CMV and donor/recipient serostatus {#sec-disc-cmv-serostatus}

The depletion hierarchy predicts that PTCy should increase CMV reactivation most when CMV-specific T-cell reconstitution matters most — i.e., when the recipient carries latent virus (R+). We assessed this in two ways.

**Meta-regression.** Neither recipient seropositivity (k = 11, p = 0.72) nor D+/R+ prevalence (k = 8, p = 0.43) significantly moderated the PTCy effect on CMV when used as study-level ecological covariates. Both analyses were severely underpowered (only 8–11 of 22 C1 CMV studies report arm-level serostatus data).

**Subgroup tabulation.** Three studies (Chorao 2024, Goldsmith 2021, Steiner 2024) reported CMV reactivation stratified by donor/recipient serostatus, contributing one study per D/R combination:

| D/R stratum | PTCy % | Comparator % | OR | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| D+/R+ | 47% | 40% | 1.34 | Chorao 2024 |
| D−/R+ | 67% | 69% | **0.91** | Chorao 2024 |
| D+/R− | 20% | 11% | 2.06 | Goldsmith 2021 |
| D−/R− | 2% | 1% | 1.89 | Goldsmith 2021 |
| R+ (pooled) | 51–68% | 29–48% | 2.4–2.6 | Goldsmith 2021, Steiner 2024 |

The **D−/R+ stratum** is the only one where PTCy did not increase CMV risk. In this configuration, the recipient carries CMV-specific memory T cells from prior infection (R+) but the donor does not (D−). Post-transplant immune reconstitution against CMV therefore relies on surviving recipient memory cells. PTCy's preferential depletion of alloreactive (recently activated) T cells may spare these quiescent, CMV-specific memory cells, whereas CNI-based regimens continuously suppress all T-cell function including memory responses. This is consistent with the mechanistic selectivity of cyclophosphamide for dividing cells.

In contrast, when the donor is seropositive (D+/R+ and D+/R−), the comparator arm benefits from donor-derived CMV-specific T cells during immune reconstitution — a pathway that PTCy disrupts by depleting the incoming donor graft during the early post-transplant window.

::: {.callout-warning}
## Hypothesis-Generating Only
All D/R stratum-specific estimates are based on k = 1 study each. The ecological meta-regressions lack power (k = 8–11 with restricted ranges). Testing whether PTCy's CMV effect genuinely differs by D/R serostatus requires individual patient data (IPD) meta-analysis, where within-study D/R interactions can be estimated without ecological bias.
:::

## Chronic GVHD: The Dominant Treatment Effect {#sec-disc-cgvhd}

The C1 moderate-severe chronic GVHD OR of 0.47 [0.38–0.57] is among the largest treatment effects in this meta-analysis, exceeding the aGVHD effect (OR 0.67). This magnitude — roughly a halving — is clinically important given that moderate-severe cGVHD is the leading cause of late morbidity and long-term immunosuppression dependence after allogeneic HCT.

The C1-vs-C2 attenuation (OR 0.47 → 0.79) mirrors the aGVHD pattern (0.67 → 0.63) and is mechanistically coherent: ATG also depletes alloreactive T cells, narrowing the differential. However, PTCy retains a residual cGVHD advantage even against ATG (90% posterior probability of OR < 1), suggesting PTCy may target the chronic alloimmune process through a partially distinct mechanism — possibly the selective elimination of alloreactive T cells that are actively dividing during the early post-transplant window, which are disproportionately represented among the long-lived T-cell clones that drive chronic GVHD.

## Infection-Related Mortality: The Risk-Benefit Resolution {#sec-disc-irm}

The IRM analysis is arguably the most clinically important finding in this report because it resolves the central risk-benefit question: *do the excess infections with PTCy translate into excess deaths?*

The answer is nuanced. In C2 (vs ATG), both the Bayesian (OR 0.90 [0.74–1.09]) and frequentist (OR 0.81 [0.66–1.00]) models agree: PTCy does not increase infection-related mortality relative to ATG. In C1 (vs CNI+MTX), the two models now agree after cohort deduplication: the Bayesian estimate is OR 1.19 [1.00–1.43] and the frequentist RE model gives OR 1.03 [0.72–1.46]. Both are compatible with no increase. The previously reported Bayesian–frequentist divergence (1.35 vs 1.01) was driven by repeated EBMT ALWP publications entering the model as independent studies.

The clinically relevant interpretation is that even under the more pessimistic Bayesian estimate, the IRM increase (OR 1.19) is far smaller than the corresponding infection rate increases (CMV OR 1.26, BSI OR 1.87, BK virus OR 2.48). This disconnect between infection incidence and infection mortality suggests that PTCy-associated infections are, as a class, manageable with contemporary antimicrobial prophylaxis and pre-emptive therapy. The net survival effect remains favourable (OS OR 0.84).

## Practical Implications {#sec-disc-practical}

The expanded analysis — now covering 10 outcomes across 3 comparisons — carries direct clinical implications:

| Domain | Finding | Intervention target | Rationale |
|---|---|---|---|
| **cGVHD** | OR 0.47 | Long-term IS tapering, QoL | PTCy profoundly reduces late morbidity |
| **OS / RRM** | OR 0.84 / 0.86 | Relapse prevention | GVL preserved; dual survival benefit |
| **CMV / BSI / BK** | Rates elevated | T-cell reconstitution, antimicrobial prophylaxis | Infections manageable; IRM not clearly increased |
| **NRM** | Mediation uncertain | GVHD control + steroid minimisation | Indirect pathway; evidence weaker post-Block-9 |
| **aGVHD** | OR 0.67 | GVHD prevention | Consistent across comparators |

::: {.callout-note}
## ATG Is Not the Solution
The C2 data show that ATG-based regimens carry at least as great a T-cell reconstitution liability as PTCy. The infection costs of PTCy are best understood as a general consequence of T-cell–depleting prophylaxis, not a PTCy-specific limitation. PTCy's advantage over ATG lies in its superior cGVHD prophylaxis and preserved GVL activity.
:::

## Limitations {#sec-disc-limitations}

### Context: relationship to prior meta-analyses

This review is substantially larger than prior work in this field. The largest comparative PTCy meta-analysis to date (Li et al. 2025, *Ann Hematol*) pooled 9 studies and 1,674 patients with only 4 studies contributing CMV data (RR 0.97 [0.76–1.22]); Tang et al. (2023, *Front Oncol*) included 6 studies from unrelated donors with CMV data too sparse for subgroup analysis (RR 0.89 [0.63–1.24]); Arcuri et al. (2019, *BBMT*) included 20 studies comparing haplo-PTCy vs URD but did not analyse infection outcomes; Gagelmann et al. (2019, *JAMA Oncol*) pooled 13 studies without infection data. A 2023 systematic literature review of PTCy vs ATG on CMV (Varma et al., *Cancers*) was qualitative only, concluding that data were scarce and no pooled estimate was possible. Thiruvadi et al. (2026, *OFID*, abstract) performed a proportion meta-analysis of csCMVi incidence after matched-donor PTCy but included no comparator arm. The present review (251 studies, 525 arms, 3,604 outcomes) therefore represents an order-of-magnitude increase in scale and, to our knowledge, the first meta-analysis to simultaneously pool infection (CMV, BSI, IFI) and transplant (GVHD, NRM, OS) outcomes across two pre-specified comparisons. This scale enables subgroup and meta-regression analyses that were not feasible in prior work. However, larger scale also amplifies certain limitations:

### Specific limitations

1. **Observational evidence.** Only 14 of 251 included studies (5.6%) are RCTs. This proportion is lower than in the smaller meta-analyses of Li et al. (2/9 RCTs, 22%) and Tang et al. (1/6, 17%), reflecting the broader inclusion criteria and the observational nature of the PTCy literature as a whole. All findings — including the depletion hierarchy — are therefore susceptible to residual confounding despite mediation modelling and multivariable-adjusted subsets.

2. **Sparse infection data.** Despite the overall corpus size, only k = 6 C1 studies contribute to BSI and k = 6 to IFI. BK virus (k = 11 C1, k = 3 C2) is better represented but has not been meta-analysed in prior PTCy reviews. By comparison, Li et al. (2025) pooled 2 studies each for bacterial and fungal infection (RR 0.86 [0.57–1.29] and RR 0.23 [0.07–0.79], respectively). Our BSI estimate (OR 1.87 [1.33–2.62]) is directionally opposite to Li et al.'s bacterial infection RR, but the two analyses are not directly comparable: Li et al. studied PTCy vs ATG in haploidentical transplants, while C1 compares PTCy vs CNI+MTX across all donor types. The depletion hierarchy predicts exactly this divergence. Nonetheless, both estimates rest on very few studies, and dedicated prospective studies of infection after PTCy are urgently needed.

3. **Single-extractor methodology.** Data extraction and ROB assessment were performed by a single reviewer (RL) for all 241 assessed studies. This departs from the Cochrane-recommended dual independent extraction used by, e.g., Tang et al. (2023), Arcuri et al. (2019), and Luo et al. (2024). A stratified spot-check by a second reviewer (BMT program director) with inter-rater reliability assessment (Cohen's kappa) is planned before publication but has not yet been completed. The ROB assessments should therefore be interpreted with this caveat in mind.

4. **Relapse-related mortality now analysed.** The post-Block-9 analysis includes RRM as a new outcome (C1 OR 0.84 [0.76–0.93], k = 38; C2 OR 0.79 [0.69–0.89], k = 10), resolving the previous limitation. The RRM benefit is steroid-independent (no M1→M2 attenuation), supporting the GVL preservation hypothesis. However, RRM is an aggregate outcome that conflates relapse incidence with post-relapse mortality; dedicated relapse incidence data would further refine the mechanistic picture.

5. **Publication bias.** RoBMA (Robust Bayesian Model-Averaging) detected strong evidence of publication bias for C1 OS (BF = 10.9) and C1 aGVHD (BF = 14.1), with moderate evidence for C2 OS (BF = 5.2) and C2 aGVHD (BF = 4.1). After bias adjustment, all model-averaged effects collapsed toward the null (P(effect) < 50% for all 9 outcomes). No meaningful bias was detected for C1 CMV, NRM, BSI, or IFI (BFs < 1). No prior PTCy meta-analysis has applied model-averaged publication bias correction; Tang et al. (2023) assessed funnel-plot symmetry qualitatively, while Li et al. (2025) did not report publication bias assessment for infection outcomes. The RoBMA default priors are deliberately skeptical (Normal(0, 1) on log-OR, 50% prior mass on the null), and the prospective/RCT subgroup analysis (below) suggests these estimates may be overly conservative — but the bias signals for OS and aGVHD warrant caution in interpreting effect magnitudes.

6. **Observational dominance and design-associated confounding.** Only 14 of 251 studies are RCTs, and very few contribute to the paired analyses (2 RCTs in C1 OS; 0 in any C2 outcome). A prospective-vs-retrospective subgroup analysis of C1 outcomes showed that OS estimates were concordant across designs (prospective OR 0.76 vs retrospective 0.78), while C1 CMV showed a complete direction reversal (prospective OR 0.68 vs retrospective 1.39, p = 0.056). Investigation revealed this reversal is driven by case-mix confounding — all 3 prospective studies used matched donors (0% haplo) and 2/3 had ATG in the comparator arm — rather than reporting bias. This underscores that apparent "study quality" effects in this literature may reflect systematic differences in donor type, conditioning, and comparator regimen rather than methodological rigour per se.

7. **Haplo proportion as effect modifier.** Meta-regression identified haplo donor proportion as a significant moderator of the aGVHD treatment effect in both C1 (β = +0.005/%, p = 0.049, R² = 22%) and C2 (β = +0.013/%, p = 0.034, R² = 47%), and of C1 OS (β = +0.005/%, p = 0.003, R² = 26%). PTCy's advantages over both comparators attenuate as haplo proportion increases, reaching null at ~100% haplo for aGVHD and OS. This is not confounded by conditioning intensity and is mechanistically coherent, but it means the pooled estimates — which average across a range of haplo proportions — may overstate or understate the effect for any specific clinical scenario. Prior meta-analyses restricted to haploidentical transplants (Li et al. 2025, Arcuri et al. 2019) were not affected by this heterogeneity but also could not detect it.

8. **M2 complete-case analysis.** Post-Block-9 M2 models use complete-case analysis for steroid adjustment (dropping arms with missing steroid data), differing from the pre-Block-9 `mi()` imputation approach due to a brms API change.

9. **C2 CMV sensitivity caveats.** The expanded-arm sensitivity conflates PTCy+ATG combination arms (which test a different clinical question — whether adding PTCy to ATG changes outcomes) with pure PTCy vs ATG comparisons. Luo et al. (2024) specifically meta-analysed ATG+PTCy combinations (14 studies) and found lower aGVHD without increased infection, consistent with our sensitivity findings. However, the convergence pattern should not be taken as evidence that PTCy+ATG combinations are clinically equivalent to ATG alone.

10. **Ecological bias in meta-regression.** All moderator analyses (haplo proportion, CMV serostatus, comparator ATG use, letermovir era) use study-level aggregates as covariates. This introduces ecological bias — the study-level association may not reflect the individual-level relationship. Testing the depletion hierarchy at the patient level, and confirming whether D/R serostatus truly modifies the PTCy–CMV relationship, requires individual patient data (IPD) meta-analysis.
